# Workflow Selection Logic

## Purpose

This document defines how to choose the right critique or teaching workflow for a painting problem.

The goal is to prevent every issue from becoming the same kind of AI correction. Different failures need different teaching artifacts.

---

## Core Principle

Choose the workflow that best helps the painter see the problem and make the next decision.

Do not default to image correction when a simpler teaching artifact would be clearer.

Prefer progressive reveal: one major visual lesson first, then secondary material only when it helps the painter act.

---

## Default Sequencing Rule

Use this sequence unless a specific painting requires a simpler path:

1. diagnostic critique,
2. scope reveal,
3. teaching critique,
4. demonstrative critique only if comparison will clarify the lesson,
5. repaint guidance.

Not every step needs a separate visible surface. The point is cognitive pacing, not ceremony.

---

## Workflow Types

### Diagnostic Critique

Use when:
- the main need is to identify what is going wrong,
- the painter needs prioritization,
- the failure mode is not yet clear.

Best for:
- first pass review,
- distance read,
- structural diagnosis,
- issue prioritization.

Output:
- concise diagnosis,
- visual consequence,
- priority,
- guiding principle.

---

### Teaching Critique

Use when:
- the painter needs to understand why the issue matters,
- the concept is transferable,
- the correction should not yet be prescribed too narrowly.

Best for:
- value grouping,
- focal hierarchy,
- edge economy,
- chroma hierarchy,
- watercolor restraint.

Output:
- principle,
- analogy or painterly explanation,
- guiding question.

---

### Prescriptive Critique

Use when:
- the visual issue is clear,
- the painter needs a next action,
- the change can be described without replacing judgment.

Best for:
- repaint planning,
- priority ordering,
- focused practice.

Output:
- what to change first,
- what to preserve,
- what to delay or omit.

---

### Demonstrative Critique

Use when:
- comparison would clarify the lesson,
- a selective correction can remain interpretable,
- the painter needs to see the effect of a decision.

Best for:
- value simplification,
- edge reduction,
- secondary chroma reduction,
- corner quieting,
- focal reinforcement.

Output:
- controlled correction,
- side-by-side comparison,
- repaint lesson.

Limit:
- use demonstration sparingly,
- avoid multiple variants unless each answers a distinct visual question,
- transition to repaint guidance once the lesson is clear.

## Failure Mode to Workflow Mapping

### Fragmented Value Structure

Preferred workflows:
- value-only workflow,
- Notan or tonal mask,
- shadow mass simplification demonstration,
- repaint planning.

Avoid:
- color-first correction,
- decorative detail edits.

---

### Weak Focal Hierarchy

Preferred workflows:
- diagnostic critique,
- side-by-side comparison,
- focal reinforcement demonstration,
- corner quieting if competition comes from edges.

Avoid:
- increasing detail everywhere.

---

### Edge Equality or Edge Clutter

Preferred workflows:
- overlay-based teaching,
- edge economy demonstration,
- side-by-side comparison.

Avoid:
- global softening that weakens structure.

---

### Excessive Chroma

Preferred workflows:
- chroma hierarchy critique,
- restrained palette comparison,
- secondary chroma reduction demonstration.

Avoid:
- dulling all color equally.

---

### Weak Atmosphere

Preferred workflows:
- atmospheric simplification critique,
- value and edge comparison,
- restrained variant exploration,
- repaint guidance.

Avoid:
- blur-like correction,
- generic mood enhancement.

---

### Watercolor Overworking

Preferred workflows:
- diagnostic critique,
- repaintability guidance,
- fresh passage planning,
- comparison with simpler wash structure.

Avoid:
- patch-style correction,
- repeated local fixes,
- digital polish.

---

## Medium-Based Selection

### Watercolor

Prefer:
- repaint planning,
- wash simplification,
- edge economy,
- value grouping,
- freshness preservation.

Use correction cautiously when the issue is paper fatigue, mud, or overworking.

---

### Medium Not Specified

Default to structural critique first:
- value,
- shape,
- edge,
- focal hierarchy,
- chroma.

Do not assume a digital art workflow.

---

## Pedagogical Intent

Choose based on what the painter needs:

- To see the problem: diagnostic critique or overlay.
- To understand the principle: teaching critique.
- To know what to do next: prescriptive critique.
- To compare an alternative: demonstrative critique.
- To repaint fresh: repaintability guidance.

---

## Stage Selection (four-stage workflow)

### In-Process vs Studio Check

Use **In-Process** when:
- the painting is clearly still in development,
- significant structural decisions are still open,
- you expect to repaint one or more passages.

Use **Studio Check** when:
- the painting feels mostly resolved,
- you are deciding whether to sign it,
- the question is "sign now or do one more thing?".

Studio Check outputs a direct signing verdict. It may suggest media additions (pen, pastel, dry brush) that In-Process would not address.

---

### Studio Check vs Archive

Use **Studio Check** before signing — corrections are still possible and edit buttons are active.

Use **Archive** after signing — the painting is closed. Archive generates a study record: strengths to repeat, weakness patterns to practice, future directions. No corrections.

---

## Selection Questions

Before choosing a workflow, ask:
- Is the failure mode clear?
- Is the problem local, regional, compositional, or better handled as a study?
- Would a correction teach more than a diagram or value study?
- What should be preserved?
- Is watercolor freshness at risk?
- What should the painter learn for the next repaint?
- Will another surface clarify the lesson or only add cognitive load?
- Is the system encouraging repainting, or inviting more AI optimization?
